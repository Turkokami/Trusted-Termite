## 1. STRIPED BARK SCORPION — *Centruroides vittatus* (Say)

**Names/ID.** Adults ~2⅜ in (60 mm), up to ~2½ in; yellowish-tan with **two broad blackish longitudinal stripes** on the upper abdomen and a **dark triangular mark on the head over the eyes**. Slender pedipalps, long slender tail; males have longer tails. Juveniles lighter, with dark brown/black pedipalp bases and terminal tail segment. Body fluoresces under UV.
— [texasinsects.tamu.edu/striped-bark-scorpion](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/striped-bark-scorpion/); [AgriLife E-362 Scorpions PDF](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Scorpions-1.pdf)

**Confused with.** Pseudoscorpions (tiny, tailless — separate AgriLife field-guide entry) and, in far-west Texas, other vaejovid scorpions. AgriLife's El Paso office documents *Paravaejovis spinigerus* (Arizona stripetail) — [elp.tamu.edu](https://elp.tamu.edu/ipm/bugs/order-scorpiones-scorpions/class-arachnida-scorpions-vaejovidae-paravaejovis-spinigerus-arizona-stripetail-scorpion-c/)

**Other scorpions present.** Texas has **~18 species**. *C. vittatus* "occurs throughout the state" and is the only species in the eastern half. Diversity rises westward: AgriLife gives counts of **4 species near Amarillo, 3 near Abilene, 5 near Fort Stockton, 8 near Fort Davis, 14 in Big Bend**.
— [mytexas.ag.tamu.edu — Scorpions: Identification, Habitat and Control in Texas](https://mytexas.ag.tamu.edu/publications/scorpions-identification-habitat-and-control-in-texas/); [texasinsects.tamu.edu/scorpionida](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/scorpionida/)

> ⚠️ **NOT FOUND — do not write about this:** a named species list for the ten target counties specifically. AgriLife gives counts for Amarillo/Abilene/Fort Stockton but not for Lubbock or Midland–Odessa. Do not name additional species for the Permian Basin.

**Life cycle.** Mate in fall, spring or early summer; **gestation ~8 months**; live birth of **13–47 young, average 31**. Young climb onto the mother's back, molt, and remain on her **3–7 days**. **5–6 molts to maturity.** AgriLife's E-362 gives lifespan ~**4 years**; the Field Guide entry says 3–8 years (some sources 20–25) — cite the 4-year figure and note variation.
— Same two AgriLife sources as above.

**Seasonal activity & habitat.** Nocturnal; hiding by day "helps them manage temperature and water balance, which are important functions for survival in dry habitats." Found under rocks, boards, debris; "distinctly associated with dead vegetation, fallen logs, and human dwellings." Active climbers — **"often are found in the attics of homes."**

**Why they enter structures — the dryness/heat trigger (this is the money quote).**
- AgriLife E-362: *"During periods of hot weather, scorpions may move into living areas to escape the high temperatures in attics."*
- Molly Keck, AgriLife Extension: *"during hot, dry weather, they may seek out water and/or a more hospitable environment."* — [AgriLife Today, 30 Jun 2018](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2018/06/30/hot-dry-summer-has-scorpions-in-south-central-texas-heading-indoors/)
- Keck again (2023 drought): scorpions pursue *"water and cooler temperatures and when they feel that temperature difference below a door or by another small crack, they can squeeze their way inside."* — [AgriLife Today, 11 Sep 2023](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2023/09/11/pests-indoors-on-the-rise-as-texas-heat-drought-continues/)
- Wizzie Brown: their flattened body "allows them to hide in small cracks and under stones, bark, wood or other objects."

So: the extension-supported framing is **heat + moisture-seeking**, entering low (under doors, cracks) and living high (attics), then dropping into living space when attics overheat. That is a genuinely regional story for the Permian Basin.

**Sting severity — accurate, non-alarmist.**
- *"The stings from Texas scorpions produce only moderate reactions in most people because the poison has little effect on the nervous system."*
- *"None of the scorpions in Texas are considered 'deadly.'"* — [Venomous Terrestrial Animals of Texas, AgriLife](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Venomous-Terrestrial-Animals-of-Texas.pdf)
- Sting causes "sharp, immediate pain"; "localized swelling often persists for days, but individual reaction varies." Local swelling and itching may last several days.
- Allergic reactions are possible as with any arthropod venom. Ice pack for pain; seek care if swelling/pain persists or breathing difficulty occurs. Higher concern for young children and allergic individuals.

> 🚩 **POPULAR-BELIEF CONFLICT.** The dangerous US scorpion is the **Arizona bark scorpion** (*C. sculpturatus*), not the Texas striped bark scorpion. No Texas species is lethal. Any West Texas pest page implying otherwise contradicts AgriLife directly.

**Management (AgriLife, non-chemical first).** Remove trash, logs, boards, stones, bricks from around the structure; mow grass short near the foundation; prune shrubs and overhanging branches; keep garbage above ground level; **never bring firewood indoors unless it goes straight on the fire**; weather-strip loose doors and windows; **plug weep holes with steel wool, copper mesh, nylon scouring pad, or screen-wire squares**; seal roof eaves and pipe penetrations; keep screens in repair. (Chemicals listed include permethrin, cyfluthrin, cypermethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, deltamethrin, bifenthrin, carbaryl; plant oils rosemary/cinnamon/clove/thyme/peppermint and pyrethrum.)

## 2. SUBTERRANEAN TERMITES — *Reticulitermes* spp.

**Which species.** AgriLife E-368 names the **native subterranean termite *R. flavipes*** (eastern subterranean) plus *Coptotermes formosanus*; "The major termite populations are in the coastal areas of Texas, but they are found throughout the state." The Field Guide adds ranges for two others that **stop well east of the Permian Basin**:
- *R. virginicus* — "east Texas to Uvalde and Tom Green Counties"
- *R. hageni* — "east Texas to Bexar and Eastland Counties"
- *Coptotermes formosanus* — Houston-Galveston and Beaumont-Port Arthur only
— [AgriLife E-368 Subterranean Termites PDF](https://extensionentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2016/06/Subterranean-termites-E368.pdf); [texasinsects.tamu.edu/termite](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/termite/)

> **Practical read for your pages:** for the target counties, write about ***Reticulitermes* / native subterranean termites**, generically or as *R. flavipes*. Do **not** claim Formosan termites. 
> ⚠️ **NOT VERIFIED — do not write about this:** the presence of ***R. tibialis*** (arid-land subterranean termite) in West Texas. It is well documented for Arizona and Colorado (below) but I found **no Texas extension source placing it in Texas**, and the Bugwood High Plains IPM page was inaccessible.

**Castes / ID.** Swarmers ~¼–⅜ in, coal black to pale yellow-brown, pale or smoky-gray wings; citybugs gives ~⅜ in, dark brown to blackish. Workers blind, wingless, white to creamy white, ¼–⅜ in. Soldiers worker-like with "well-developed brownish heads with strong mandibles"; only **1–5%** of the colony.
**Termite vs ant swarmer:** termites lack a pinched waist and have four wings of **equal length**; ants have a pinched waist and longer front wings.
— [citybugs ENT-2002 FAQ](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/factsheets/household/termites/ent-2002/); E-368

**Swarming season & triggers — regionally specific.**
- *"Swarming begins in South Texas in January and February; in the Panhandle region of Texas, swarms do not occur until April and May."* — [urbanentomology.tamu.edu](https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/urban-pests/termites/native/); E-368
- *"Environmental factors such as heat, day length, and moisture trigger the emergence of swarmers."*
- Statewide, citybugs says swarms are "most commonly seen between the months of February and May"; flights occur **during daylight hours**, typically after rain.
→ **For your counties, April–May is the defensible swarm window.**

**Colony.** 2–4 years to produce swarmers (E-368 says mature in 3–5 years); mature native colonies "about 250,000 workers," though many are smaller.

**Mud tubes.** Built of "particles of soil or wood and bits of debris held together by salivary and fecal secretions." Function: protection while foraging above ground, bridging masonry barriers, and carrying moist soil into the structure. **"The presence of shelter tubes and mud within galleries indicates an active infestation."**

**Slab vs pier-and-beam (E-368, verbatim substance).**
- *Slab:* "Termites enter buildings through wood that is in direct contact with the soil and by building shelter tubes over or through cracks in foundations." Bath traps and utility penetrations are key vulnerabilities; removable access hatches recommended; post-construction treatment often requires drilling.
- *Pier-and-beam:* minimum **18-inch clearance between floor joists and soil**, **at least 12 inches between floor beams and soil**; cover **at least 70% of crawlspace soil** with 6-mil polyethylene; cross-ventilate at **1 sq ft of vent per 150 sq ft of crawlspace**, vents opposite each other and near corners.

**Conducive conditions.** Termites "become dehydrated easily" and need soil contact **or** another moisture source — "defective plumbing, leaky roofs, leaks from air conditioning condensers, or poorly maintained gutters." Importantly: *"if moisture is available, termites can be away from the soil for extended times,"* explicitly correcting an older belief. Cultural measures: grade sloping away on all sides; **soil level at least 6–8 in below the top of the foundation**; remove all wood-to-soil contact (fence posts, stair carriages, trellises, door facings); remove form stakes, wood scraps, stumps; **avoid dense shrubbery against the house** because it raises humidity and blocks inspection of the foundation line; separate porches/steps from soil by **at least 6 in** on solid concrete.

**Evidence.** First sign is usually swarmers or **discarded wings on windowsills, in spider webs, or near indoor lights**. Mud tubes on piers, foundation walls, slabs, near utility openings. Damage follows the grain, attacks soft spring growth, and galleries are "lined with mud or may have a pale, spotted appearance from soft fecal material."

**Economic framing (E-368).** ~$500 million damage/year in Texas; an unprotected home has roughly a **70% chance of termite damage within 25 years** (state-level figure — do not present as West Texas-specific).

### Critical West Texas confusable: DESERT TERMITE — *Gnathamitermes tubiformans*
"Common in south and west Texas," conspicuous **May–September** on shortgrass pasture; builds **"fragile mud tubes and sheets of mud around the surfaces of herbaceous and woody plants, litter, dung, fence posts and similar objects."** Workers ⅜ in, creamy white with brown heads. **Does not tunnel into wood; no structural damage; "medically harmless to man and animals."** Feeds on living/dry forage grasses; a rangeland issue, worse on overgrazed or drought-stressed ground.
— [texasinsects.tamu.edu/desert-termite](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/desert-termite/); [mytexas.ag.tamu.edu — Desert Termites](https://mytexas.ag.tamu.edu/publications/desert-termites-gnathamitermes-tubiformans/)

> 🚩 **This is the single most useful regional differentiator you can put on a West Texas termite page.** Mud sheeting on fence posts, dead grass and dung in a Gaines County pasture is almost certainly desert termite, not a structural threat. Sourced, checkable, and it demonstrates competence.

## 3. RED HARVESTER ANT — *Pogonomyrmex barbatus* Smith

**ID.** Workers **¼–½ in, red to dark brown, squarish heads, no spines on the body**; 12-segmented antennae without a club; a **psammophore** (row of hairs under the head) for carrying loads. Eleven *Pogonomyrmex* species in Texas, "ten found statewide"; **"Seven of these species are found only in far west Texas."**
— [AgriLife E-402 Red Harvester Ants PDF](https://aes.tamu.edu//files/2014/06/Red-Harvester-Ants.pdf); [texasinsects.tamu.edu/red-harvester-ant](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/red-harvester-ant/); [urbanentomology.tamu.edu](https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/urban-pests/ants/red_harvester/)

**Mound.** **A single central opening**, with small pebbles deposited on the surface, and **"no vegetation within a 3- to 6-foot circle around the central opening"** (E-402). The Urban Entomology page gives a larger cleared disc: "averages about 12 feet in diameter but may range from 3 feet to 35 feet."

**Foraging trails.** Distinct radiating trails, also cleared of vegetation; "may extend outward for 200 feet, becoming less distinct farther away from the nest." Collect seeds (alfalfa, grasses, mesquite) and dead insects; store seeds in the nest.

**Reproduction/seasonality.** Winged males and females swarm and mate, "especially following rains"; males die, females dig a burrow and lay a few eggs; larvae pupate in cocoons.

**Sting.** *"Worker ants can give a painful, stinging bite, but are generally reluctant to attack. Effects of the bite can spread along lymph channels and can be medically serious."* Urban Entomology notes people are "often attacked and stung while playing" in recreational areas.

**Do they enter structures? — NO.** AgriLife E-402, verbatim: **"They do not invade homes or structures."** This is a clean, quotable answer.

**Texas horned lizard.** E-402: harvester ants "are not nearly as common today as they were during the earlier 1900s," and this matters because they are **"a major source of food for the rapidly disappearing and threatened Texas horned lizard."** Contributing causes given: red imported fire ant predation and insecticide use. E-402's management advice leads with **"not controlling these ants, especially in areas inhabited by the few remaining horned lizards."** Where control is needed: habitat disruption by regular discing and mowing, or a labelled bait.

> 🚩 **HIGH-VALUE CONTRAST.** AgriLife's *first* management recommendation for a stinging ant is **don't treat it**. For a licensed operator in horned-lizard country this is both defensible and differentiating. Note the Texas horned lizard is a state-listed threatened species — treat mounds only where there is a genuine human-contact hazard.

## 4. RED IMPORTED FIRE ANT — *Solenopsis invicta* Buren

**ID.** Workers **1/16–3/16 in (1.5–5 mm)**, reddish-brown with darker gaster. **Worker size variation within one colony is a key diagnostic:** *"Variation in size is a distinguishing characteristic."* Ten-segmented antennae with a two-segmented club; double pedicel.
— [fireant.tamu.edu/learn/fire-ant-identification](https://fireant.tamu.edu/learn/fire-ant-identification/); [How can I tell if I have fire ants?](https://fireant.tamu.edu/manage/how-can-i-tell-if-i-have-fire-ants/); [citybugs ENT-2013](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/factsheets/household/ants-house/ent-2013/)

**Fire ant vs harvester ant — the decisive tests:**

| | Fire ant | Red harvester ant |
|---|---|---|
| Worker size | Variable, 1/16–3/16 in | Uniform, ¼–½ in (much bigger) |
| Mound | Fluffy worked soil, **"no opening in the center like most ant mounds"** — they enter via underground tunnels | **Single central opening**, pebbles on surface |
| Cleared area | Not characteristic | Bare disc 3–6 ft (to 12+ ft) plus cleared trails |
| Disturbance | "dozens to hundreds of reddish-brown worker ants crawl up vertical surfaces" | "generally reluctant to attack" |
| Enters buildings | Yes | **No** |

Sources as above plus [AgriLife B-6043](https://research.entomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/28/2013/09/b6043.pdf). Also note bigheaded ants are the commonest fire-ant confusion: *"imported fire ants do not have workers with larger heads."* — [fireant.tamu.edu/learn/native-ants](https://fireant.tamu.edu/learn/native-ants/)

**Mounds.** Built in "almost any type of soil, but prefers open, sunny areas such as pastures, parks, lawns, meadows and cultivated fields"; up to 18–24 in tall in pasture, a few inches in turf. Whitish objects at the top are brood.

**Do they enter structures? — Yes.** B-6043: *"Fire ants from colonies close to homes and other buildings sometimes forage indoors for food and moisture, particularly during the hot, dry, summer months. Entire colonies occasionally nest in wall voids or rafters, or behind large appliances."* Colonies may move indoors during floods or drought. FAQ page repeats: indoor movement seeking "food and water," particularly in drought — directly relevant to Permian Basin summers.

**Reproduction.** Newly mated queen ⅜ in; a worker-fed queen lays **800–1,000 eggs/day** (Field Guide says ~200/day; use B-6043's figure or give a range). Mature colonies >200,000. Polygyne (multi-queen) colonies show no inter-mound territoriality, producing dramatically higher mound densities.

**Sting/medical — accurate framing.** Burning, itching, then a **white fluid-filled pustule** in a day or two — this pustule is essentially diagnostic. Multiple stings produce a circular pattern (one ant stings repeatedly). About **1%** of the population is hypersensitive ([fireant.tamu.edu FAQ](https://fireant.tamu.edu/manage/faq/)). B-6043: *"relatively few deaths from fire ant stings have been documented"* relative to bees and wasps; but severe chest pain, nausea, sweating, difficulty breathing, serious swelling or slurred speech = emergency.

### Western range limit in Texas — **the strongest regional finding in this whole brief**

- fireant.tamu.edu FAQ: fire ants *"now infest more than the eastern two-thirds of the state, and some urban areas in western Texas."* Western expansion "depends largely on availability of surface or ground water." Field Guide: **"Fire ants infest the eastern two-thirds of Texas."**
- USDA ARS potential-range modelling identifies **rainfall, irrigation and cold** as the determinants, with irrigated land supporting populations where natural rainfall would not. — [USDA ARS, Texas range expansion](https://www.ars.usda.gov/southeast-area/gainesville-fl/cmave/imported-fire-ant-and-household-insects-research/docs/potential-united-states-range-expansion-of-the-invasive-fire-ant/texas/)
- On the AgriLife-hosted **Texas Imported Fire Ant Quarantine Area map**: **Ector, Midland, Howard and Martin counties appear on the quarantine list**, plus a defined **infested area of the City of Lubbock** (bounded by Hwy 27 east, Ursuline St north, Milwaukee St west, 98th St south). **Gaines, Yoakum, Terry, Dawson and Andrews do not appear.** — [texashelp.tamu.edu quarantine map PDF](https://texashelp.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/04/texas-imported-fire-ant-quarantine-area-map.pdf)

> ⚠️ **Two caveats you must respect.** (a) On repeat retrieval the document was **inconsistent about Martin County** — treat Martin as uncertain. (b) The AgriLife-hosted map carries no printed effective date and sits in a 2017 upload folder; the current federal quarantine is the **APHIS interactive map, which I could not fetch** ([aphis.usda.gov/plant-pests-diseases/ifa/ifa-quarantine-map](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/plant-pests-diseases/ifa/ifa-quarantine-map)). **Verify county status against the live APHIS map before publishing county-by-county claims.**

> 🚩 **POPULAR-BELIEF CONFLICT.** Statewide Texas pest content treats fire ants as ubiquitous. In your service area they are at the **western edge of the range**, present chiefly in irrigated urban and municipal landscapes — Midland/Odessa/Big Spring and pockets of Lubbock — and likely absent or marginal on dryland caliche in Gaines, Yoakum, Terry, Dawson and Andrews. That is honest, checkable, locally credible differentiation.

## 5. BROWN RECLUSE — *Loxosceles reclusa* Gertsch & Mulaik

**ID (all five features required, per UC IPM).** **Six eyes in three pairs (dyads)**; uniformly coloured abdomen with fine hairs; **no spines on the legs**; uniformly coloured legs (no bands); **body not more than ⅜ in**. Legspan about a quarter to a half-dollar. The violin marking points backward from the head. *"The eye pattern is the best way to eliminate almost all suspect non-recluse spiders."*
Wizzie Brown, AgriLife: the violin markings *"are sometimes faint and can be similar to other non-venomous spiders… The size, color and eye-pattern are the best indicators."* And: *"Only one other spider group in Texas has that eye pattern, and they are not venomous."*
— [UC IPM Pest Note 7468, Brown Recluse](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/legacy_assets/pdf/pestnotes/pnbrownrecluse.pdf); [AgriLife Today, 30 May 2023](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2023/05/30/brown-recluse-spider/); [citybugs ENT-3003](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/factsheets/biting-stinging/others/ent-3003/)

**Commonly misidentified as recluses:** cellar spiders, pirate spiders, spitting spiders, wolf spiders, sac spiders, ground spiders, **male black widows and false widows** (UC IPM). Any brown spider with banded legs, leg spines, or eight eyes is not a recluse.

**Actual Texas distribution.** Native range: *"the south central Midwest from southeastern Nebraska to southwestern Ohio and south through Texas to north Georgia"* (UC IPM). AgriLife's Field Guide lists **four to five *Loxosceles* in Texas**, and this is where regional accuracy matters:
- *L. reclusa* — brown recluse; **"no records yet from the Lower Rio Grande Valley"**
- ***L. blanda* — Big Bend recluse, "from West Texas"**
- *L. devia* — Texas recluse, "from Central and South Texas"; **"may be the most common species in Texas"**
- *L. apachea*, *L. rufescens* — "scattered locations across the state"
— [texasinsects.tamu.edu/recluse-spider](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/recluse-spider/)

> ⚠️ **NOT FOUND — do not write about this:** a county-level or West-Texas-specific distribution map for *L. reclusa*. AgriLife/DSHS say recluses occur "throughout the State" without regional resolution ([Texas DSHS Spiders](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/disease-surveillance-epidemiology-section/zoonosis-control/animal-bites/venom/spiders)). **Safest accurate phrasing:** "Recluse spiders occur across Texas, but more than one species is involved and identification to species requires an expert; treat any recluse-type spider the same way."

**Where they shelter.** Indoors: cluttered closets, garages, crawlspaces, attics, wall voids, under insulation, dry narrow crevices, behind baseboards, in boxes, under furniture. Outdoors: under old boards, stacked firewood, stones, rock ledges, under bark of dead trees, discarded junk. Nocturnal hunters; they do not catch prey in webs.

**Bite frequency and severity — this is where popular belief is worst.**
- UC IPM: ***"About 10% of brown recluse bites cause moderate or greater tissue damage and scarring, but the vast majority only result in inflammation and heal well without medical intervention."***
- UC IPM, documented case: a Kansas family collected **2,055 brown recluses in six months**; *"only once in 10 years had anyone shown evidence of a bite."*
- AgriLife citybugs: *"brown recluse spiders frequently coexist with humans in homes without incident."*
- Wizzie Brown, AgriLife: ***"There has never been a confirmed death attributed to the brown recluse spider."*** And bites *"are rarely as serious as some stories or images portray on the internet"* — *"Some photos presented as brown recluse spider bites are horrible, but anyone can put information and photos on the internet."*

**Misdiagnosis — the documented scale.** UC Riverside (Vetter) compiles: South Carolina 1990, **940 physicians reported 478 presumed recluse bites** against **one verified brown recluse** in the scientific literature for the state; Florida, **124 diagnosed bites across 31 counties** against **11 verified specimens in 100 years** of arachnological records; four western states over 41 months, **216 diagnoses** against **35 verified spiders ever found** — *"not one bite victim ever submitted a brown recluse."* Summary: *"doctors from non-endemic areas will diagnose several recluse bites in a month, more recluse bites than spiders have ever been found in their entire states."* A toxicologist quoted by UC IPM estimated **60%** of "brown recluse bite" diagnoses came from areas with no known *Loxosceles*.
Conditions misdiagnosed as recluse bites include **MRSA and other staph/strep infections**, necrotizing fasciitis, herpes simplex, shingles, sporotrichosis, vasculitis, thromboembolism, Lyme, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, tick and insect bites, warfarin reaction, poison ivy, chemical burns, diabetic ulcers, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, pyoderma gangrenosum, lymphoma, pressure ulcers.
— [spiders.ucr.edu — Causes of Necrotic Wounds other than BRS Bites](https://spiders.ucr.edu/causes-necrotic-wounds-other-brs-bites); UC IPM PN 7468

AgriLife's own bite-diagnosis fact sheet adds: *"Spider bites are rare, and thus can be ruled out in cases of people with multiple lesions or bites."* — [citybugs ENT-3006, Diagnosing Mysterious "Bug Bites"](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/factsheets/biting-stinging/others/Ent-3006/)

> 🚩 **THE MOST VALUABLE CONTRAST IN THIS BRIEF.** A licensed operator who says plainly "most recluse bites heal without treatment, no confirmed death has ever been attributed to this spider, and multiple simultaneous 'bites' are almost never spiders" is both correct and trustworthy in a market saturated with necrosis photos.

**Management (AgriLife + UC IPM, non-chemical first).** Remove clutter; tape storage boxes shut on all seams; move beds away from walls and keep bedding off the floor; store clothing in sealed containers; shake out stored clothing or run it through a hot dryer for 45 minutes; check shoes; wear leather gloves handling firewood or stored goods; seal doors and windows; shield/redirect outdoor lighting; **sticky cards along walls, in closets and under furniture** for monitoring and control; vacuum spiders, webs and egg cases; reduce prey insects. AgriLife notes heavy infestations warrant a professional.

## 6. BLACK WIDOW / WIDOW SPIDERS — *Latrodectus* spp.

**Species in Texas — four:** southern black widow *L. mactans*, **western black widow *L. hesperus***, northern black widow *L. variolus*, and brown widow *L. geometricus*. AgriLife's El Paso office documents *L. hesperus* for far west Texas.
— [AgriLife Spiders PDF (2025)](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Spiders-1.pdf); [texasinsects.tamu.edu/southern-black-widow-spider](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/southern-black-widow-spider/); [elp.tamu.edu — L. hesperus](https://elp.tamu.edu/ipm/bugs/class-arachnida-arachnids/class-arachnida-araneae-theridiidae-latrodectus-hesperus-western-black-widow-a-3/)

> Write "black widow (*Latrodectus* spp.)" for West Texas. Both *L. mactans* and *L. hesperus* are plausible; separating them needs an expert. **Do not assert a single species.**

**ID.** Adult female shiny black, body ~⅜ in (AgriLife's Spiders PDF gives "average 1½ inches long," which is legspan, not body — use the Field Guide's ⅜ in body). **Red hourglass on the underside of the abdomen**, sometimes reduced to spots; may have a red spot behind the spinnerets. Males much smaller (~3/16 in) with narrower abdomens and **white lines along the sides**; immatures similar, spiderlings orange-and-white becoming black through successive molts. Eight eyes in two rows.

**Web & habitat.** Strong, sticky, **irregular mesh** — not an orb — built low and in dark, weather-sheltered spots, with a small silken retreat tent. The spider **hangs upside down, showing the hourglass**. AgriLife lists exactly the harbourage a West Texas technician inspects: **woodpiles, garages, cellars, shrubbery, crawlspaces, rain spouts, termite bait stations, gas and electric meters**, plus outbuildings, holes in dirt embankments, and under stones or logs. Egg sacs rounded, ~½ in, hanging in the web; 25–400+ eggs per sac; spiderlings emerge ~4 weeks.

**Seasonality.** *"Adults of both sexes have been found throughout the year in buildings."* — i.e. **no clean off-season indoors**; outdoor encounters concentrate in warm months.

**Temperament.** *"Quite timid… attempts to escape rather than to attack"* when disturbed.

**Bite severity — accurate.** Neurotoxic. AgriLife: "moderate to intense pain throughout the body that could last from 2 to 3 days," plus tremors, nausea, vomiting, leg cramps, abdominal pain; more dangerous for children, the elderly, and people with health problems; seek emergency care — antivenom exists. Texas DSHS notes the venom is "reportedly 15 times more toxic than the venom of the prairie rattlesnake" **but only minimal quantities are injected**, and that antivenom "carries anaphylaxis risk and is rarely used."
Clinical outcome data (US National Poison Data System, ~2,600 *Latrodectus* exposures/year; series of 23,409): **65% minor effects, 33.5% moderate, 1.4% major (life-threatening)**; *"Most patients can, and should, expect a full recovery"*, usually within 24–48 hours.
— [Texas DSHS Spiders](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/disease-surveillance-epidemiology-section/zoonosis-control/animal-bites/venom/spiders); [StatPearls, Black Widow Spider Toxicity (NCBI)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK499987/)

> 🚩 **SOURCE CONFLICT — FLAGGING THIS EXPLICITLY.** AgriLife's *Venomous Terrestrial Animals of Texas* states, verbatim: **"Less than 5 percent of reported bites result in death."** This is an outdated and badly misleading figure — it is irreconcilable with NPDS data showing 1.4% of exposures reach *major* (life-threatening, not fatal) severity and with the near-total absence of US widow fatalities in recent decades. **Do not reproduce this sentence on a customer-facing page**, even though it is technically attributable to AgriLife. Use the DSHS framing plus the NPDS severity breakdown instead. (Cite: [Venomous Terrestrial Animals of Texas](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Venomous-Terrestrial-Animals-of-Texas.pdf) — recorded here so you know the conflict exists and where it comes from.)

**Management (AgriLife Spiders PDF).** *"Sanitation and physical removal are the best way to manage most spiders."* **"The best way to eliminate spiders is a good hand vacuum"** — removes spiders, webs and egg cases together. Install/repair weather stripping; move firewood and construction materials away from the house; prune shrubs and trees off the structure; **direct light away from doorways, turn lights off when not needed, use yellow bug lights**; seal all cardboard storage box seams with wide tape.

## 7. HOUSE MOUSE — *Mus musculus* L.

**ID.** ~5–7 in total including a 3–4 in tail; ~½ oz; light brownish to grey. Distinguished from **deer mice** (*Peromyscus*) by the overall grey coat and a **relatively hairless tail** — deer mice have bicoloured, moderately to well-furred tails and white bellies. This distinction matters in West Texas: deer mice are the hantavirus reservoir, house mice are not.
— [UC IPM Pest Note 7483, House Mouse](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/legacy_assets/PDF/PESTNOTES/pnhousemouse.pdf)

**Minimum gap — sourced figures (three independent, converging).**
- UC IPM: mice can *"squeeze under gaps ¼ inch tall, and through openings 3/8 inch wide."* Exclusion standard: **seal all gaps and openings larger than ¼ inch.**
- Nebraska Extension (via ICWDM): *"mice only need slightly more than a ¼-inch gap"*; rats *"slightly more than a ½-inch gap."* — [ICWDM rodent-proof construction PDF](https://icwdm.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/rodent-proof-construction-structural.pdf)
- AgriLife School IPM: openings *"as small as 1/4 inch in diameter"*; *"a mouse can squeeze through a hole the size of a pencil diameter."* — [schoolipm.tamu.edu — IPM Action Plan for Rodents](https://schoolipm.tamu.edu/forms/pest-management-plans/ipm-action-plan-for-rodents/)

**→ Use ¼ inch (about the diameter of a pencil) as your headline figure. It is the AgriLife number and the Nebraska number.**

**Reproduction.** **5–10 litters per year, about 5–6 pups each**; gestation **18–21 days**; sexually mature at **5–6 weeks** (can extend to 12); lifespan 9–12 months, occasionally to 3 years (UC IPM). AgriLife School IPM gives **6–10 litters of 5–6, 18–21 day gestation**. ICWDM: 5–10 litters/year, litters of 5–6, gestation 19–21 days, mature in 6–10 weeks. — [ICWDM House Mouse Biology](https://icwdm.org/species/rodents/house-mice/house-mouse-biology/)

**Feeding & evidence.** Nibblers: eat up to **20% of body weight daily** in roughly **200 small meals each night** (UC IPM); ~1/10 body weight/day and up to 8 lb/year, ~0.4 oz water/day (ICWDM). This is why bait-shy behaviour differs from rats and why many small bait/trap placements beat few large ones. Evidence: droppings, gnaw marks (**mouse gnaw marks ~1/12 in / 2 mm; rat ~1/6 in / 4 mm**, UC IPM Rats), tracks/footprints, and a characteristic **musky odour** in long-standing infestations.

**Edge behaviour.** AgriLife School IPM names it: **thigmotaxis** — rodents travel linear pathways, "corners between walls and floors" and "along the base of foundations." UC IPM: set traps behind objects, in dark corners, **close to walls so mice pass directly over the trigger**; space traps **no more than ~10 feet apart** in active areas.

**Home range.** *"Seldom venture more than 30 feet from their nest sites and food supply"* (UC IPM); ICWDM: an area averaging **10–30 feet in diameter**, returning to the nest up to **40 times a day**. This is the operational reason mouse control is about density of devices in a small radius, not perimeter treatment.

**Seasonal movement indoors.** ICWDM: mice *"are most likely to invade structures as temperatures cool in the fall."* AgriLife adds a second, drought-driven trigger: rodents *"are driven to buildings to seek water and escape extreme temperatures"* in hot, dry conditions ([AgriLife Today, Sep 2023](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2023/09/11/pests-indoors-on-the-rise-as-texas-heat-drought-continues/)). **In the Permian Basin that gives you two pushes a year: late-summer drought and the first cold fronts.**

**Behavioural note that separates mice from rats.** Mice *"quickly detect new objects but do not fear them"* and will "almost immediately enter bait stations and sample bait" (ICWDM) — the opposite of rat neophobia. Practical consequence: mouse traps work fast; rat traps need pre-baiting.

**Management.** Seal openings >¼ in with **metal or concrete**, not gnawable material — UC IPM specifically excludes foam, plastic and wood; **24-gauge ¼ × ¼ in hardware cloth to exclude mice**, 19-gauge ½ × ½ in for rats, galvanized sheet metal 24 gauge or heavier (ICWDM). NMSU adds that **steel wool, latex, plastic, rubber and wood are all gnawable and should not be relied on** ([NMSU Guide L-209](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_l/L209/index.html)) — note this conflicts with AgriLife's steel-wool suggestion for scorpion weep holes; steel wool is acceptable against arthropods, not as a permanent rodent seal. Store food in metal, glass or heavy plastic with tight lids; keep stored materials **18 in off the floor with space from walls** (NMSU). Snap traps set at right angles to the wall; use enough traps to make the trapping period short and decisive.

## 8. ROOF RAT & NORWAY RAT — *Rattus rattus*, *Rattus norvegicus*

**Which occur here.** Texas Tech's *Mammals of Texas* (Natural Science Research Laboratory):
- **Black/roof rat:** *"Common over most of Texas, especially in towns."* — [TTU NSRL, *Rattus rattus*](https://www.depts.ttu.edu/nsrl/mammals-of-texas-online-edition/Accounts_Introduced_Rodentia/Rattus_rattus.php)
- **Norway rat:** *"Widespread in Texas but not so common in the southern half of the state as the black rat."* — [TTU NSRL, *Rattus norvegicus*](https://www.depts.ttu.edu/nsrl/mammals-of-texas-online-edition/Accounts_Introduced_Rodentia/Rattus_norvegicus.php)

> ⚠️ **NOT FOUND — do not write about this:** any source giving roof rat vs Norway rat prevalence **for the Permian Basin or South Plains specifically**. AgriLife's own roof-rat commentary is explicitly about **east** Texas: roof rats are *"quite common in our urban communities in east Texas and throughout the south"* ([citybugs, Rodents in attics](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/2016/01/05/rodents-in-attics/)). ICWDM's national account states *"most of the states in the US interior are free of roof rats"* ([ICWDM Roof Rats](https://icwdm.org/species/rodents/roof-rats/)). **Correct phrasing: both species occur in Texas towns; write generically about "rats" and identify by evidence on site rather than asserting which species dominates locally.**

**Identification (UC IPM Pest Note 74106, Rats):** — [UC IPM Rats PDF](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/legacy_assets/PDF/PESTNOTES/pnrats.pdf)

| | Roof rat | Norway rat |
|---|---|---|
| Build | "sleek, agile" | "large, robust" |
| Weight | 5–10 oz | 7–18 oz |
| Tail | "extends at least to snout," uniformly dark | "extends to the middle of the skull," dark above/pale below |
| Ears | "long enough to reach eyes when folded over" | "don't reach eyes when folded over" |
| Muzzle | pointed | blunt |
| Belly | grey to white | mostly grey |
| Droppings | ~½ in, **pointed ends** (ICWDM) | larger, blunt ends |

**Behaviour and nesting — the operationally important split.**
- **Roof rats:** agile climbers; nest **above ground** — "attics, wall voids, ceiling voids, and cabinets," and outdoors in trees and shrubs; strong **nest-site affinity**; forage **up to 300 ft or more**; prefer fruits, tree nuts, berries, snails, young birds and eggs.
- **Norway rats:** burrowers — burrows "along building foundations, beneath rubbish or woodpiles"; occupy **basements and ground floors**; forage **100–500 ft** from the nest; prefer cereal grains, meat, fish, nuts; strong swimmers; hoard food and can translocate bait (AgriLife School IPM).
- AgriLife School IPM: roof rats travel *"up to 300 feet in search of food,"* Norway rats *"approximately 150 feet."*

**Gap size.** Both species: *"squeeze beneath a door with only a 1/2-inch gap"* (UC IPM). AgriLife School IPM: rats need **½ inch**. AgriLife's attic article: a roof rat gets through *"a gap under a door as narrow as 1/2 inch, or a hole the size of a quarter."* But note UC IPM's exclusion standard is stricter than the biological minimum: *"All holes larger than 1/4 inch should be sealed"* against both rats and mice, because juveniles are the concern.

**Evidence.** Droppings; gnaw marks ~1/6 in; **"smudge marks caused by rats rubbing their fur against beams, rafters, pipes, and walls"** (rub marks are a rat sign, not a mouse sign); burrows; nesting material; nocturnal movement on utility lines or fence tops; for roof rats specifically, the "pitter-patter of tiny feet" in ceilings. TTU NSRL for roof rats: they "usually frequent attics, rafters, and crossbeams," leaving a "dark-colored layer of grease and dirt" along runways.

**Neophobia.** AgriLife School IPM: *"both roof and Norway rats are leery of new things in their environment"* — requires patience and pre-baiting. **Directly opposite to the house mouse.** This is a good, sourced technical point for a pest library.

**Reproduction.** Roof rats: 3–5 litters/year, up to 8 young (UC IPM); TTU NSRL gives ~7 per litter averaged, 21-day gestation, breeding peaks Feb–Mar and May–Jun in Texas, sexual maturity ~3 months. Norway rats: 4–6 litters/year, 20+ weaned annually (UC IPM); TTU NSRL: gestation 21–23 days, litters 2–14 averaging 7–8, breeding year-round with a fall/winter slowdown, lifespan 2–3 years. AgriLife School IPM: roof rat 3–4 litters of 4–8; Norway rat 8–9 pups per litter.

**Exclusion specs.** 18-gauge, ½ in (13 mm) mesh galvanized expanded metal or equivalent — **never lightweight "chicken wire"**; seal around pipes, cables and wires; screen ground-level windows; spark arrester on chimneys; tight-fitting exterior doors sealed at the threshold. Roof rats can climb vertical pipes up to 3 in diameter, jump 36 in vertically and 48 in horizontally; Norway rats burrow at least 36 in straight down (ICWDM). AgriLife's field advice: seal with cement, foam, steel wool or a commercial product such as Xcluder, then trap — and *"the most common mistake with snap traps is not putting out enough traps."*

**Health.** Hantavirus is carried by the **deer mouse** (*Peromyscus maniculatus*), **not** house mice or commensal rats — CDC identifies the deer mouse as the host of the most common HPS-causing hantavirus in the US, with **38% of people who develop respiratory symptoms dying**. Transmission is by inhaling aerosolized urine/droppings/saliva; never sweep or vacuum droppings — wet them with a 10% bleach solution (1.5 cups bleach per gallon of water), wait an hour, double-bag; use an N-100/HEPA respirator, not a painter's mask.
— [CDC Hantavirus](https://www.cdc.gov/hantavirus/about/index.html); [NMSU Guide L-209](https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_l/L209/index.html)

> 🚩 **Relevant regional nuance.** Deer mice are a genuine consideration in West Texas outbuildings, barns and rural structures. This is a real, CDC-sourced risk that is *distinct* from the house mouse — don't blur them, and don't attach hantavirus to house mice.

## 9. GERMAN COCKROACH — *Blattella germanica* (L.)

**ID.** **½–⅝ in**, light brown, with **two dark parallel stripes on the pronotum**. Winged in both sexes but they do not fly. Nymphs have wing pads and two dark stripes running the entire length of thorax and abdomen.
— [AgriLife ENTO-037, Cockroach Biology and Management](https://extensionentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/cockroach-biology-and-management-compressed.pdf); [urbanentomology.tamu.edu German cockroach](https://urbanentomology.tamu.edu/cockroaches/german-cockroach/)

**Why it is different from every other roach you'll see here — three sourced reasons:**
1. **It does not live outdoors in Texas.** ENTO-037, verbatim: German cockroaches *"do not live outdoors in Texas"* and *"spend their lives indoors."* Urban Entomology: they *"cannot tolerate cold temperatures"* and *"are mainly an indoor pest."* **→ A German cockroach infestation was carried in — in cartons, groceries, appliances, secondhand furniture, or from an adjoining unit. It is never a perimeter problem, and perimeter spraying does not address it.**
2. **The female carries the ootheca until hatch.** She retains the egg capsule until the nymphs are ready to emerge, sharply raising survival (Urban Entomology) — so contact insecticide applied to surfaces largely misses the next generation.
3. **It lives in cracks.** *"About 75 percent of its life in narrow cracks and crevices, from 1/32 to 3/16 inch wide"* (ENTO-037) — which is why space sprays and total-release aerosols fail: *"total-release aerosols (bug bombs) generally do not eliminate cockroaches because they do not penetrate the cracks where most cockroaches hide."*

**Reproduction rate.** **30–40 nymphs per egg capsule**; nymph to adult in **50–60 days** at normal indoor temperatures; optimum ~**80 °F**; *"the most prolific of indoor cockroaches and can produce a generation in about 100 days."* Roughly 3–4 generations per year indoors.

**Harbourage & attraction.** Dark shelters **near moisture and food** — kitchens and bathrooms; under sinks, behind plumbing, behind and under appliances, in cabinet voids, behind baseboards. As populations grow and food runs short, they disperse to bedrooms and other rooms — **dispersal into non-kitchen areas is a severity indicator**. Daytime sightings: *"If you see cockroaches during the day, it usually means there are a lot of them around."*

**Health significance.** ENTO-037: *"one of the most widespread insect pests to public health in urban homes, apartments, and restaurants. They transport germs and are associated with allergies and asthma."* NIEHS: *"Researchers have found a link between the presence of cockroaches and an increase in the severity of asthma symptoms."* — [NIEHS, Dust Mites and Cockroaches](https://www.niehs.nih.gov/health/topics/agents/allergens/dustmites)
Practical consequence AgriLife draws: vacuum with **a HEPA filter** "because cockroach harborages often contain allergens."

**Why sanitation and harbourage beat spraying (AgriLife's own hierarchy).** Control requires *"inspection, sanitation, exclusion, and the use of low-toxicity insecticides."* Sticky-card monitors along walls and cabinet edges to find harbourage. Clean floors and counters, **fix water leaks to remove moisture sources**, seal the cracks they hide and travel in. Vacuuming removes both roaches and their food residues from under stoves and behind refrigerators. A hair dryer can be used to flush roaches out of harbourage for inspection. **Baits are the primary chemistry:** at least one placement **within 1–2 feet of every suspected harbourage**; *"many small, pea-sized placements are more effective than fewer, larger ones"*; if populations persist beyond **2–3 weeks**, rotate to a different bait with a new active ingredient. Boric acid dust "remains one of the more effective cockroach control insecticides," applied so lightly that residues are barely visible.

> 🚩 **POPULAR-BELIEF CONFLICT.** Customers expect a spray. AgriLife's position is that the German cockroach lives in crevices a spray cannot reach, breeds in capsules a spray cannot kill, and that bombs specifically do not work. Sanitation, crevice sealing and correctly placed gel bait are the treatment — the spray is the least effective part.

## 10. AMERICAN COCKROACH AND OTHER LARGE PERIDOMESTIC ROACHES

All from [AgriLife ENTO-037](https://extensionentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/cockroach-biology-and-management-compressed.pdf) unless noted.

**American cockroach — *Periplaneta americana*.** Also called "waterbugs" or "palmetto bugs." **The largest cockroach in Texas, 1½–2 inches.** Reddish brown with **tan to light-yellow banding outlining the pronotum**. Both sexes fly. Young nymphs greyish brown, becoming reddish brown after a few molts. Development to adult averages **600 days**; adults live 1–2 years.

**Where they come from — and why they appear indoors.** They are outdoor and commercial-building insects: **"compost piles, ground cover plants, hollow trees, mulch, old stumps, palm fronds, woodpiles, sewer manholes, and underground water meters"**, and they are *"one of the most common cockroaches in sewer systems."* They *"live mostly outdoors but may move indoors in search of food or water,"* entering through **poorly sealed doors, roof soffits, and floor drains** — *"American and oriental cockroaches use floor drains as common points of entry into buildings."*

**Prevention, verbatim.** *"Keep P-traps filled with water to create a barrier between the sewer and the home or business."* *"Check the threshold seals under doors and ensure that roof soffits are screened."*

> 🚩 **KEY CONTRAST.** A single 1½-inch reddish roach in a West Texas bathroom at night is almost always a **wanderer from a drain, meter box or sewer line** — a plumbing and exclusion problem, not an infestation. A ½-inch striped roach in a kitchen is a German cockroach and a genuine infestation. Getting customers to understand this difference is worth a whole page.

**Oriental cockroach — *Blatta orientalis*.** Dark brown to black, ~1¼ in, **does not fly**, wings stay short in both sexes. Egg to adult 575–600 days; adults 1–6 months. Prefers **cooler, ground-level sites — basements, crawlspaces, wet areas**. Uses floor drains to enter.

**Smokybrown cockroach — *Periplaneta fuliginosa*.** 1¼–1½ in, dark brown to black, wings extend beyond the abdomen in both sexes, antennae as long as or longer than the body. Nymphs black with **two white bands and white-tipped antennae**. **"Common outdoors, especially in the more humid areas of Texas"** — prefers protected, moist, warm sites away from moving air: ground cover, mulch, palm fronds, soffits, attic eaves, tree holes. *Likely limited in the semi-arid Permian Basin outside irrigated landscaping — treat as secondary.*

**Turkestan cockroach — *Blatta lateralis* (also *Shelfordella/Periplaneta lateralis*) — the one to watch.** Males brownish-yellow, ½–⅞ in, pronotum with a pale margin; females shiny dark reddish brown with short wings that have **pale outer bases** (this is how you separate a female from an oriental cockroach). Native to Asia, introduced to California, **"expanding its range into Texas."** AgriLife: *"It has become one of the most common outdoor cockroaches in El Paso, where it is found in compost, leaf litter, and potted plants; in expansion joints of sidewalks; and around water meter boxes."* It is well suited to arid and semi-arid conditions. — ENTO-037; [texasinvasives.org](https://www.texasinvasives.org/pest_database/detail.php?symbol=32)

> ⚠️ **NOT FOUND — do not write about this:** confirmed Turkestan cockroach records in Midland, Ector, Lubbock or any of the ten target counties. Texas Invasives lists El Paso only. **You may accurately say it is established in El Paso and expanding in Texas, and that arid West Texas habitat (water meter boxes, sidewalk expansion joints, potted plants) suits it — but do not claim it is present in the Permian Basin.** (AgriLife published a 2024 piece titled "The Turkestan Cockroach: An Emerging Pest in Texas" at citybugs.tamu.edu; that page would not load for me — worth a re-check before publication.)

## 11. FIELD CRICKETS — the West Texas autumn phenomenon

**Species.** The *Gryllus assimilis* complex — **"collectively referred to as black field crickets, or field crickets."**
— [AgriLife citybugs ENT-2003, Cricket control in the fall](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/factsheets/household/misc-house/ent-2003/); [texasinsects.tamu.edu/field-cricket](https://texasinsects.tamu.edu/field-cricket/)

**Life cycle.** Females lay **150–400 eggs** in firm soil in fall, about two weeks after maturing. *"Eggs remain in the soil throughout the winter and hatch the following spring."* Nymphs take **approximately three months** to develop. Adults then fly and mate.

**What drives the outbreak — the actual mechanism.**
- *"Cricket outbreaks occur when large numbers of nymphs complete their development and embark on nighttime mating flights."*
- *"The largest cricket outbreaks seem to occur during years of dry springs and summers."*
- Numbers peak **"in August and September when a summer drought is broken by rainfall and cooler weather"** — AgriLife's proposed mechanism is reduced fungal disease pressure during the dry period.
- Heaviest flights **"usually last only 1–2 weeks."**
- Wizzie Brown (2024): cool fronts trigger activity; the swarming window typically runs 4–6 weeks before decline; year-to-year intensity varies unpredictably. — [AgriLife Today, 24 Sep 2024](https://agrilifetoday.tamu.edu/2024/09/24/why-crickets-swarm-in-the-fall/)

> 🚩 **This mechanism — dry spring/summer, then a September rain and cool front — describes the Permian Basin/South Plains climate pattern almost exactly.** You can present it accurately as *why* West Texas gets bad cricket years, using AgriLife's stated mechanism, without claiming a West Texas-specific study.

**Critical fact that changes the sales conversation.** Field crickets **"do not breed indoors or establish permanent indoor infestations"** — they are **"only accidental indoor invaders."** House crickets (*Acheta domesticus*) are the exception that can persist indoors.

**Attraction to lights.** The dominant driver. Wizzie Brown: *"You'll notice that they tend to congregate around the front doors of businesses, at gas stations, car dealerships."* Crickets congregate at bright dusk-to-dawn lighting.

**Damage & nuisance.** Indoors they may stain or feed on clothing, drapes, wall coverings. Outdoors during severe outbreaks *"dead crickets quickly pile up, causing odors."* No medical significance reported.

**Management — AgriLife's stated priority order.**
1. **"Lighting reduction is the most important single step in a cricket control program."** Replace with **low-pressure sodium vapor lamps** and **yellow incandescent "bug lights"**; turn off unnecessary lights.
2. **Exclusion:** seal door thresholds, **weep holes**, soffits; fit **door sweeps with nylon bristles or rubber seals**.
3. Insecticides (baits, perimeter liquids — carbaryl, permethrin and others) as a supplement only.

> ⚠️ **NOT FOUND — do not write about this:** any Texas A&M AgriLife or other extension account of a **mass cricket emergence specifically in Lubbock, Midland, Odessa or the Permian Basin.** I searched AgriLife Today, citybugs, ipm.tamu.edu, schoolipm, the El Paso County IPM site and county agrilife.org sites. AgriLife's documented cricket-invasion reporting covers **East and Central Texas, Austin, the Hill Country, Dallas and northeast Texas** ([Texas IPM Program, 2012](https://ipm.tamu.edu/2012/07/cricket-invasion-hits-parts-of-east-and-central-texas/); NBC News 2007 quoting AgriLife's Mike Merchant — again Austin/Hill Country/Dallas, **not** West Texas).

## 12. PAPER WASPS AND YELLOWJACKETS

All from [AgriLife E-239, Paper Wasps, Yellowjackets and Solitary Wasps](https://extensionentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2010/05/E239-Paper-wasps-Yellowjackets-and-solitary-wasps-1.pdf) unless noted.

**Paper wasps — *Polistes* spp.** (*P. exclamans*, *P. carolina* named for Texas).
- **ID:** ¾–1 inch, reddish-orange to dark brown, often with yellow markings.
- **Nest:** *"The comb, which hangs from a single filament, is usually oriented downward and consists of a single tier of hexagonal-shaped cells"* — **open-celled, no paper envelope**, and this is the single best field diagnostic. Typically **20–30 adults, rarely more than 200 cells.**
- **Location:** most frequently **under the eaves of houses**; also attics, garages, storage sheds, barns, shrubbery, trees and other protected sites.
- **Colony cycle:** overwintered queens emerge in spring and found nests; by midsummer usually one active egg-laying queen per nest; males and new queens produced late summer/fall; **queens overwinter in sheltered places — "high structures, such as the peaked attics of houses, chimneys and tall buildings."**
- **Nest reuse:** *"New nests are constructed every year, often in the same general location where nests were built the previous season."* → **Removing an old nest does not prevent return; the site is what attracts them.** This is a genuinely useful, sourced customer point.

**Yellowjackets — *Vespula* spp.** (*V. squamosa*, southern yellowjacket, documented in Texas).
- **Nest:** *"primarily ground nesters, but they also construct aerial nests"* — subterranean in gardens, flower beds, pastures, roadside embankments; aerial in trees, **under eaves, in wall voids of buildings**, open garages, sheds, porches, abandoned furniture. Enclosed in a paper envelope.
- **Colony size:** *"may contain up to 45 levels of combs and 20,000 adult workers."*
- **Risk driver:** scavenging behaviour — *"yellowjackets are a menace around parks, camps and suburban sites."*
- **Nest reuse:** *"Abandoned nests rapidly decompose and are not reused the next year,"* **but** in sheltered locations *"perennial colonies may be maintained by both queen(s) and workers into the following year."*

**Baldfaced hornet — *Dolichovespula maculata*** — "the only 'hornet' reported in Texas." Round or pear-shaped grey/brown paper nest up to 3 ft long, 2–4 horizontal combs inside. **"Hornets are less likely to attack than paper wasps or yellowjacket wasps. Hornets typically nest high in trees or in other remote locations, where they pose no threat to humans."**

**Sting risk — accurate scale.** AgriLife's *Venomous Terrestrial Animals of Texas* gives the national context: about **25 deaths per year in the US from bee and wasp stings**, and **"only about one death from bees or wasps"** per year in Texas. The risk is real but concentrated in sting-allergic individuals and in mass-envenomation events.

**When a nest is genuinely urgent — defensible criteria from the sources:**
- A **yellowjacket** nest (ground or wall void) near a doorway, walkway, play area, mower route, or where scavenging brings wasps to people/food — large colony, aggressive, ground-nest disturbance is the classic multiple-sting scenario.
- A **paper wasp** nest at head height on an entry door frame, porch ceiling, patio furniture, or a gate/handle a person must reach past.
- Any nest where a household member has a known sting allergy.
- **Not urgent:** a baldfaced hornet nest high in a tree (AgriLife: "pose no threat"); mud dauber nests — **females do not defend their nests**, and the structures *"can simply be removed by hand with a putty knife"*; cicada killers — *"Control is rarely warranted for this otherwise beneficial insect,"* males cannot sting.

**Management.** Treat **"in the late evening when most wasps have returned to the nest."** Underground yellowjacket nests respond best to **insecticide dust**. *"In late fall or early winter, after the first hard frost, most yellowjacket nests die; abandoned nests can be plugged up (with or without treatment) to prevent further infestations next year."* Actives listed: pyrethrins, resmethrin, tetramethrin, bifenthrin, cyfluthrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, permethrin, esfenvalerate, carbaryl.

## 13. MOSQUITOES — semi-arid, playa-lake, no-permanent-stream setting

**Primary source:** [AgriLife, Mosquitoes and the Diseases They Transmit](https://agrilifeextension.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/mosquitos-and-the-diseases-they-transmit-1.pdf)

**Texas has at least 85 mosquito species**, in four principal genera with distinct breeding strategies:
- ***Aedes*** — container **and** floodwater breeders
- ***Psorophora*** — floodwater breeders
- ***Culex*** — permanent and transient pool breeders
- ***Anopheles*** — permanent and transient pool breeders
— confirmed by [AgriLife AES, Mosquitoes of Texas](https://www-aes.tamu.edu/mosquitoes-of-texas/) (85 species; also lists *Culiseta*, *Coquillettidia*, *Mansonia* and others)

**Which matter in a region with playas and no permanent streams — the reasoning, with sources:**

**Floodwater breeders (*Aedes*, *Psorophora*)** exploit *"flood plains, salt marshes, smaller sites, even animal footprints"* and *"develop rapidly during heavy rains."* This is exactly the playa mechanism: TPWD documents **~19,300 playas in the Texas High Plains — "the highest density of playas in North America"** — averaging just over 15 acres, ~87% under 30 acres, **"primarily filled by rainfall"**, and going through **"frequent, unpredictable, wet/dry cycles."** — [TPWD, Panhandle Playa Lakes](https://tpwd.texas.gov/landwater/land/habitats/high_plains/wetlands/playa.phtml)
*Psorophora* flight range is exceptional: **5–10 miles** — so a playa filling several miles from a subdivision is a legitimate source.

**Container breeders (*Aedes*)** use *"artificial containers, discarded tires, tin cans, flower pots, cemetery vases, roof gutters, tree holes."* These are the short-range species: *Ae. aegypti* / *Ae. albopictus* fly **under 100 yards to 100–300 yards** — meaning container mosquitoes on a property came from **that property or the neighbour's**. This is the single most actionable fact for a source-reduction sales conversation.

***Culex*** breed in transient and permanent pools and are the **West Nile vectors** — AgriLife names *Culex quinquefasciatus* (southern house mosquito) as the WNV vector, and the urban St. Louis encephalitis vector in Texas. CDC identifies *Culex* generally, imaged as *Cx. quinquefasciatus*. — [CDC West Nile Virus](https://www.cdc.gov/west-nile-virus/about/index.html)

**Egg desiccation tolerance and hatch-on-flooding — the mechanism you asked for.** AgriLife: *"some mosquito eggs can remain dormant in dry conditions for many months to 2 years."* Eggs darken to dark brown/black within hours of being laid; *"In warm water, the eggs may hatch in 2 to 3 days."*
→ **The accurate statement for a West Texas page:** floodwater and container *Aedes*/*Psorophora* lay drought-resistant eggs on damp soil or container walls above the waterline; those eggs survive the dry months; a single playa-filling rain or a refilled container floods them and triggers synchronised hatch. That is why mosquito pressure here is an **event**, days after rain, rather than a continuous season.

**Egg-to-adult development in hot conditions.** AgriLife: **"some mosquitoes can develop from egg to adult in 8 to 10 days"** under optimal conditions — larvae 4–10 days, pupae 1–10+ days. **Use 8–10 days as the sourced hot-weather figure**, and the practical rule that standing water present for more than about a week can produce adults.

> ⚠️ **NOT FOUND — do not write about this:** a species-level mosquito survey attributing specific species to the ten target counties, and any AgriLife statement naming *Culex tarsalis* as the West Texas WNV vector. The DSHS 2019 arbovirus report mentions *Cx. tarsalis* only in connection with **Western equine encephalitis**, not WNV. Do not name *Cx. tarsalis* as your local WNV vector.

**Regional public-health data you *can* use — and it's striking.** Texas DSHS 2019 arbovirus surveillance: WNV incidence was **highest in Public Health Region 9/10 at 1.2 cases per 100,000 (18 cases)** and **second-highest in Region 1 at 0.7 per 100,000 (6 cases)**; several other regions had fewer than 5 cases and **Regions 7, 8 and 11 reported none.** — [DSHS 2019 Arbovirus Activity Report](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/sites/default/files/IDCU/disease/arboviral/westnile/Reports/2019-DSHS-Arbovirus-Activity-Report-Final.pdf)
DSHS region assignments confirm this maps onto your service area: **Region 1** = Lubbock, Gaines, Yoakum, Terry, Dawson; **Region 9** = Andrews, Martin, Howard, Midland, Ector. — [DSHS, Texas County Numbers and Public Health Regions](https://www.dshs.texas.gov/center-health-statistics/texas-county-numbers-public-health-regions)

> 🚩 **POPULAR-BELIEF CONFLICT (strong, and locally counterintuitive).** People assume semi-arid West Texas has minimal mosquito-borne disease risk. In 2019 the two DSHS regions covering your ten counties had the **highest and second-highest West Nile incidence rates in Texas**, while several wetter regions reported none. Present as one year of surveillance data, not a trend — but it is real, official, and it reframes the whole mosquito conversation for this market.

**WNV risk, stated accurately (CDC).** **~80% of infected people develop no symptoms**; ~20% develop febrile illness; **less than 1% develop severe neuroinvasive disease**; **about 10% of those with severe neuroinvasive illness die.** Season June–October, diagnoses peaking late August to early September. No vaccine or specific treatment; bite prevention is the control. — [CDC WNV Signs & Symptoms](https://www.cdc.gov/west-nile-virus/signs-symptoms/index.html)

**Also relevant here:** **dog heartworm** (*Dirofilaria immitis*), transmitted by suspected *Culex*, *Aedes* and *Anopheles* species, with AgriLife recommending veterinary prevention **year-round in Texas.**

**Management.** Source reduction first: drain gutters, containers, bird baths, tyres, tarps, "any small areas holding water." Biological: mosquito fish, *Bacillus* products (Bti/Bs) for water that cannot be drained. Personal protection: repellents, protective clothing.

## 14. IPM FRAMEWORK FOR STRUCTURAL PEST CONTROL — the actual regulatory and extension framework

### A. Texas Department of Agriculture — the regulatory definition (this is the one with legal weight)

TDA defines IPM as: **"an ecologically based strategy that focuses on pest prevention by combining techniques such as biological control, habitat manipulation, cultural practices, and the use of pesticides."**

TDA's six **required elements** of a Texas school IPM program (verbatim):
1. "A school board approved IPM policy"
2. "A monitoring program with established thresholds to justify corrective action"
3. **"Preferential use of non-chemical strategies and low risk pesticides for the control of pests"**
4. "A system to maintain records of all pest control related activities"
5. "A plan for educating and informing school district employees of their roles in the IPM program"
6. "A school district appointed IPM Coordinator to implement the school district's IPM program"

Plus: *"Pesticide controls should only be used after monitoring indicates established pest thresholds have been met."* All pesticides used must be classified **Green, Yellow or Red** per Texas Administrative Code **Rule 7.204**.
— [TDA, Structural Pest Control Service — School IPM](https://texasagriculture.gov/Regulatory-Programs/Pesticides/Structural-Pest-Control-Service/School-Integrated-Pest-Management)

> This is directly quotable and gives your library pages regulatory grounding — TDA is the agency that licenses the company.

### B. Texas A&M AgriLife — the urban/structural definition and hierarchy

- **citybugs (Insects in the City), the structural-pest arm:** *"IPM is the use of multiple control tactics to control pests. IPM programs seek to rely on a blend of non-chemical, biological and chemical methods to control pests."* And: *"when pesticides are used, they are used judiciously and only when the cost of not treating exceeds the environmental and economic cost of the pesticide."* The **IPM pyramid** shows that *"a well balanced IPM program will rely largely on good cultural and sanitation practices and rely least on pesticides."* Process: *"IPM starts by confirming the identity of a pest, then using knowledge of pest's biology to design the best, most effective and environmentally safe control strategy."* — [citybugs, What is IPM?](https://citybugs.tamu.edu/factsheets/ipm/what-is-ipm/)
- **Texas School IPM program:** IPM is *"a strategy of managing pests using multiple control tactics that provide the best control with the least cost and environmental impact."* *"A good IPM program attempts to make schools less hospitable to pests by modifying the environment, like improving sanitation, and eliminating pest harborage sites, along with using the lowest impact pesticides as necessary."* — [schoolipm.tamu.edu](https://schoolipm.tamu.edu/)
- **Explicit six-step sequence (AgriLife Landscape IPM — the clearest stepwise statement I found):** 1. "Scout and monitor for pests and damage" → 2. accurate pest identification → 3. "Recognize and diagnose plant damage" → 4. establish an action threshold → 5. implement appropriate IPM practices → 6. **"Evaluate the success of your program and make needed adjustments to prevent pest problems."** — [landscapeipm.tamu.edu, What is IPM?](https://landscapeipm.tamu.edu/what-is-ipm/)
- **The four-pillar prevention framework for urban pests (AgriLife):** eliminate **food** ("Store food in hard, reusable containers with airtight, fitted lids"), eliminate **water** ("Fix leaky and dripping pipes, faucets, and roofs"), eliminate **shelter** ("Seal cracks and crevices with silicone caulking and copper mesh"), and coordinate at community level. — [AgriLife, Common Urban Pests](https://extensionentomology.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/8/2011/05/Common-urban-pests.pdf)
- **Texas IPM Program framing:** *"The goal of IPM is not to eradicate pests, but to eliminate pest problems…"* — [ipm.tamu.edu](https://ipm.tamu.edu/)

> ⚠️ **Note:** AgriLife's fullest structural IPM manual, *An Introduction to IPM in Schools: A Manual for Facilities Maintenance Professionals*, sits behind AgriLife Learn and its content was not retrievable. The six-element TDA list plus the landscape six-step sequence plus the citybugs pyramid together give you a complete, citable framework.
